What is Ilisus?

A place where learning moves.

Ilisus is turning static stories into dynamic experiences. You step into the story, make the choices, and see what follows. Each experience puts a map, a timeline and real sources together, so a war, a city, a book or a life becomes something you can walk through instead of something you only read about.

The name
Ἰλισός
A fair resting place, full of summer sounds and scents.Plato, Phaedrus, c. 370 BCE

The Ilisos is a small river that ran just outside the walls of ancient Athens. In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates leaves the city and walks along its bank to talk with a friend, in the shade of a plane tree. It is one of the best known scenes in the history of teaching.

What matters about the scene is where it happens. Learning was not locked inside a hall. It was outdoors, in conversation, and it moved at the pace of the people walking. We took the name because that is the kind of learning we want to build.

What we believe

The humanities deserve better than a static page.

The big ideas of human history are about people, choices, and consequences. They are best understood by living inside them for a while.

  • Ideas should not sit still.A book can tell you about the decisions Washington faced. An experience lets you make them, and feel why they were hard.
  • Seeing and doing teach differently.Maps, paintings, letters, and choices let people see how events fit together in time and place, and remember them.
  • New technology should serve the question.The tools to build these experiences have never been better. We want to use them to help people understand universal ideas such as liberty, duty, power, faith, money, and belonging.
  • The humanities matter more as tools get stronger.The more capable our technology becomes, the more we need people who can think about what is worth doing with it.
How it works

Read it, see it, decide.

Our flagship, Liberty, follows the American Revolution moment by moment. Pick a moment and the map flies there. Old maps drawn by the people who fought are stretched to fit the modern ground, so the armies, roads and forts sit where they really were.

Every moment has a short essay built on the record, with sources you can check. Other experiences let you make the decisions yourself, like the sample on our home page, and then show you what history says came next.

The idea came from listening to a biography of George Washington and not knowing where anything was happening. A map you can explore on your own time fixes that. That spark became the whole collection.

The goal is simple: people who leave with better questions than they came with.

Learning

Learning takes diligence.

Understanding is earned, not handed over. Ideas are meant to be absorbed three ways: through reading, through discussion, and through experience. Reading gives depth. Discussion tests what we think we know. Experience makes it last.

Our experiences are built for the third, and they point back to the first two. They do not replace the book or the classroom. They send you back to both with better questions.

What informs how we build

  • Experiential learning. Kolb's cycle of doing, reflecting, generalizing and trying again.[1]
  • The ICAP framework. Learning deepens as people move from passive to active, constructive and interactive engagement.[2]
  • Multimedia learning. People learn more from words and pictures together than from words alone.[3]
  • Retrieval practice. Recalling and deciding, even when it feels harder, produces more durable learning.[4]
AI

AI, and the gift of fire.

In Greek myth, Prometheus, whose name means forethought, gave fire to humanity. In Plato's telling, fire and craft were not enough. People also needed justice and respect for one another to use them well.[5]

AI is a new kind of fire. It lets a small team build what once took years of specialist work. It can also state false things with confidence and repeat the biases of what it learned from.[6] So we use it with forethought.

Our AI Statement

We use AI to build our experiences. We check what it produces against credible sources, and we work to remove error and bias. People decide what is true, what matters and what is worth telling. When we get something wrong, we want to hear it, and we will correct it. The humanities are human first.
